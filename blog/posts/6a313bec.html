My Writing vs Hemingway — A Humbling Data Confession

In the previous post, we put Hemingway and Faulkner side by side and watched the data confirm what a century of literary criticism had already settled: two titans, opposite rhythms, same era. That was fun. That was comfortable. You get to play critic from a safe distance, admiring the gulf between two Nobel laureates like a spectator at a tennis match.

This post is different. This post is personal.

I ran my own writing through the analyzer and put it next to Hemingway's. Not because I think I'm in his league — I'm emphatically not — but because I wanted to see, in hard numbers, what separates a native literary master from a kid learning English in a Chinese classroom.

A bit of context. I was born in 1995. The writing samples I used are from around 2013 — I would have been seventeen or eighteen, a high school student in China grinding through English compositions. These aren't creative works. They're assignment responses: short essays, book reports, "describe your summer vacation." The kind of writing millions of students produce every year, all over the non-English-speaking world, dutifully following the rules they were taught.

Here's what the data looks like.

Chart 1: The Long Shadow of Textbook English

The chart tells the story before you even read a word of analysis. My curve lags well to the right of Hemingway's — my sentences are, on average, much longer than his.

At first glance, this seems backwards. Shouldn't a non-native speaker, working with a smaller linguistic toolbox, produce shorter sentences? Shouldn't the native speaker with full command of the language be the one unfurling elaborate constructions?

But that logic gets it exactly wrong. The real dynamic is this:

When you learn English in a Chinese classroom, you are not learning the living language. You are learning a formal blueprint. Every sentence must have a subject and a predicate. Every verb must have its object where grammar demands one. The copula must be present. The subordination must be signaled with a proper relative pronoun. Nothing can be dropped. Nothing can be implied. The sentence is a machine, and every part must be bolted on.

Here are a few lines from my own 2013 writing, transcribed honestly:

"It is widely acknowledged that environmental protection has become an issue of great concern in contemporary society. In my opinion, there are several measures that can be taken to address this problem effectively."

That is not English. That is an English-shaped object. It is grammatically flawless and completely dead. A native speaker, given the same thought to express, might write:

"We need to take care of the planet. Here's what we can do."

Twelve words versus thirty-two. But the difference isn't just word count. The difference is that one version sounds like a human being talking, and the other sounds like a textbook reciting itself.

Another example. I wrote:

"It is necessary that we should make every effort to cultivate the habit of reading extensively, for the reason that reading can broaden our horizons and enrich our knowledge."

A native speaker might say:

"Read more. It opens your mind."

Five words. Five. And it says everything my thirty-word sentence said, plus conviction.

This is what the chart is capturing. Hemingway's sentences are short not because he lacked the vocabulary for longer ones, but because he had mastered the art of leaving things out. He could drop the scaffolding — the "it is widely acknowledged that," the "in my opinion," the "for the reason that" — because he trusted the reader to fill in the gaps. I, on the other hand, couldn't leave anything out, because I didn't know what was allowed to be left out. I had never heard English spoken in a café or a kitchen or a bar. I had only ever seen it diagrammed on a blackboard.

In 2013, I simply did not know how English-speaking people actually spoke. I couldn't write a sentence like "Guess I'll head out, then" — the subject is dropped, the future tense is collapsed into a contraction, the whole thing floats on implication. To my eighteen-year-old brain, that wasn't a real sentence. A real sentence needed a subject ("I"), a modal verb ("will"), a main verb ("go"), and a destination ("out"). "I will go out then." Seven words. Stiff as a board. But correct. Always correct.

And so my sentences ran long — not because I had more to say, but because I didn't know what I was allowed to leave unsaid.

Chart 2: The Vocabulary You Don't Have

If Chart 1 was about rhythm, Chart 2 is about range — and this one hurts a little more to look at.

In the high-frequency zone — the first few hundred most common English words — my curve runs to the right of Hemingway's. I used common words less heavily than he did. At first this might sound like a good thing: maybe I was reaching for more sophisticated vocabulary? But that's not what's happening. The real explanation is simpler and more sobering: Hemingway and I lived in different worlds and talked about different things. He wrote about war, fishing, drinking, bullfighting, love, death. I wrote about environmental protection and the importance of reading. Our subject matter pulled from different pockets of the language. His high-frequency words were the bedrock of lived experience; mine were the prefabricated vocabulary of academic writing prompts.

But the truly revealing part of Chart 2 comes later — in the middle and tail sections of the curve.

Look closely and you'll see it: my curve has flat segments, followed by sudden vertical jumps. Hemingway's curve rises smoothly, continuously. Mine stutters.

What does a flat segment mean? It means a range of words where I used nothing. Not a single word from that frequency band appears in my writing. The curve goes horizontal across an entire swath of the English vocabulary — words I simply didn't know, or knew passively but never reached for, or didn't think belonged in "proper" writing.

And then — jump — the curve shoots up. That's me finally landing on a word I did know and using it. Probably using it repeatedly, in fact, since each jump represents a word that suddenly covers a chunk of the cumulative distribution all by itself.

Hemingway's smooth curve is the signature of a fully-stocked mind. His vocabulary was large enough that every frequency band contributed its share — no deserts, no sudden cliffs. My staircase curve is the signature of a learner with a small, patchy vocabulary 😅. I had islands of competence surrounded by oceans of ignorance. The words I knew, I knew well and used often. The words I didn't know — well, they might as well not have existed.

I'm better now than I was in 2013. Living in an English-speaking environment, reading more widely, writing more freely — all of that has smoothed out some of those flat segments. But I'd be lying if I said the staircase has turned into a ramp. It hasn't. Not yet. There are still entire neighborhoods of the English lexicon where I've never walked, streets I don't even know the names of.

The Gift of Data

Looking at your own writing next to Hemingway's is not an exercise in self-flattery. It's closer to standing in front of a mirror under harsh fluorescent light — every asymmetry visible, every flaw lit up. But that's also the gift.

In 2013, I didn't know what I didn't know. I thought my English was fine because my grammar was correct and my teachers gave me good marks. The charts would have told a different story — a story about sentences that were long for the wrong reasons, and a vocabulary that was more gaps than substance. I just didn't have the tools to see it.

Now I do. And now I can do something about it.

We live in an era of absurd educational abundance. Every book, every podcast, every film, every conversation with a native speaker is a free English lesson if you're paying attention. The resources that were locked behind paywalls and geography when I was seventeen are now available to anyone with an internet connection. I have no excuse. It's time to get serious about rebuilding my English — not for an exam this time, but for real.

Living in a time like this and not learning properly would be, frankly, an insult to the opportunity. So here goes.

Why I Built This Tool

I once dreamed of becoming a writer.

I wanted to write stories that moved people. The kind that make someone stop mid-sentence, unable to speak. I worked hard at it. In university, I started publishing fiction in magazines and online journals. I won awards. I earned money from my writing. Everything was heading in the right direction.

It was also during that time that I encountered literary theory — a lot of it.

Book after book, I kept finding the same thing. Ideas I had felt since childhood, in a vague, half-formed way — ideas I had never spoken aloud — were printed there in black and white. Literature is not only the art of imagery. It is also the art of form. The length of a sentence. The rise and fall of sound. These are not merely a writer's "personal style." They are literature itself. They are the point.

What surprised me even more: the writers who proposed these ideas, and the historical writers they cited as influences, were exactly the writers I loved. I tracked down their essays on language, one by one, and read them. Each time I finished, I had the same strange feeling: for thousands of years, my favorite writers had been agreeing with me.

I wasn't following them. They seemed to be waiting for me.

And so a quiet resolution took shape. I might not have extraordinary talent for creative work. I might never produce a truly great novel. But I would make my mark on literary theory. At the very least, I would bring this school of thought into the light. I would not let it molder in forgotten books.

At the time, I was learning to program. I started designing software to analyze text at scale.

Looking back, I was not thinking clearly. I couldn't see the obvious. I did many strange things. I forced myself to use Scheme, for no practical reason — I just thought the language looked beautiful. I insisted on deriving every numerical analysis formula by hand, refusing to use any existing libraries. Working through textbooks line by line, I hand-rolled my own spline interpolation algorithm. In retrospect, this was madness.

Nobody can do everything well. I certainly don't have the brains for it.

Eventually I came to my senses. I grabbed Python's scipy library. I had a working prototype very quickly. The interface was rough. The features were bare. But it ran. You could drop a text in, and it would spit out sentence length distributions and word frequency curves. I stared at those crooked charts for a long time.

Then I kept loosening up. When AI tools arrived, I used them to turn the prototype into an online version. It got an interface. It got interactivity. It got multiple languages. It became WritingStyleAnalyzer.com.

The tool itself is not complicated. But it works. It works well.

I hold one belief firmly. No matter how advanced AI becomes, human beings will always need to express themselves. And wherever there is expression, there is a need for beautiful expression. The reason is simple. Every sentence you send out into the world shapes who you are in someone else's mind. You don't get a second chance.

The biggest problem today is that people cannot see their own problems.

They cannot see how ugly their sentences are. They cannot see the hollow filler phrases clinging like limescale to every paragraph they write. They cannot see that they have written for a lifetime without ever producing a single clean, forceful sentence. This blindness is not a failure of taste. It is a lack of tools. If nobody puts the data in front of your eyes, you keep believing your writing is fine.

And in the years ahead, as AI-generated content floods into every corner of human expression, this problem will only deepen. When rough human prose and smooth AI prose mix together, it will become even harder to tell which sentences are yours and which are not — harder to see your own true face.

That is why this website needs to exist.

It is not an ambitious project. There is no server farm, no deep learning model, no business plan. It is a web page. You put words in, it gives you two curves. That's all. But those two curves let you see yourself.

I hope it becomes a base. Failing that, a ruin. Something that later travelers come across — the ones born for words, the ones groping in the dark like I once did. They stop here. They see some remains. They take some strength from them. Then they keep walking.

That is enough.

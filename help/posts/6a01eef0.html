What the Charts Actually Mean?

In the previous post, we looked at how to use the two charts through comparison. Now let's open the black box. What does a single point on either curve actually represent? Here's the math, the algorithms, and how to read what you're seeing.

Chart 1: Sentence Length CDF

After splitting your text into sentences, we count how many words each sentence contains. Let p(i) be the proportion of sentences that have exactly i words. The cumulative distribution is:

$$P(n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p(i)$$

In plain English: P(n) is the fraction of sentences that contain n words or fewer.

Take the highlighted point on the chart above as an example. The coordinates read (10, 0.62), which means: 62% of the sentences in your text have 10 words or fewer. The remaining 38% are longer. The curve rises fastest where sentences concentrate — a steep segment at low word counts signals a preference for short sentences.

The line is drawn as a step function (horizontal-then-vertical) because sentence length is discrete: you cannot have 10.5 words in a sentence. Between integer values, the proportion stays flat until the next sentence length is reached.

How sentences are split

A naive approach would be to split on every period. But that would break on Mr. Smith, U.S. policy, or e.g. this example. Our sentence splitter uses a two-phase strategy:

  1. Identify non-boundaries. Common abbreviations (Mr., Mrs., Dr., Prof., e.g., i.e., etc.), initials (J. K. Rowling), and numeric contexts (3.14, $5.99) are recognized and temporarily replaced with placeholder markers so they are not mistaken for sentence ends.
  2. Split on real boundaries. Only true sentence-ending punctuation — .!? followed by whitespace and a capital letter — triggers a split. The placeholders are then restored to their original form.

This is why the sentence counts you see are reliable even for text dense with abbreviations, dialogue, or academic citations.

Chart 2: Word Frequency CDF

Each word in your text is first lemmatized — reduced to its dictionary base form. Then each lemma is looked up in a reference frequency table (the COCA corpus, containing the 4,380 most common English words ranked from 1 = most frequent to 4,380 = least frequent). Words not found in the table are treated as even rarer.

The same cumulative logic applies, this time accumulated over frequency ranks. G(r) is the fraction of words in your text that fall within the r most common English words.

Take the highlighted point as an example. The coordinates read (890, 0.88), which means: 88% of the words you used are among the 890 most common words in English. The other 12% are rarer. A curve that rises early and stays high suggests vocabulary drawn from everyday language; a curve that lags suggests a higher proportion of uncommon words.

How words are lemmatized

Word frequency analysis collapses if run, runs, and running are counted as three separate words. Lemmatization solves this by mapping every inflected form to a single base lemma. Our lemmatizer combines two approaches:

  1. Irregular mappings. A lookup table handles high-frequency irregulars: went → go, better → good, mice → mouse, and hundreds of others. Contractions are also resolved: don't → do, I'll → I.
  2. Suffix stripping. Regular inflections are removed in a carefully ordered pipeline: -ing, -ed, -s, -es, -ies, -ly, -er, -est, and more. Each stripping step checks against a NO_STRIP protection list — words like interesting (not "interest") or visited (not "visite") — to prevent over-lemmatization.

A silent-e recovery rule ensures that making becomes make (not "mak") and filed becomes file (not "fil"). The entire operation runs locally in your browser — no word ever leaves your device.

Why step lines?

Both charts use a step-line rendering rather than smooth curves. This is mathematically correct: both sentence length and frequency rank are discrete integer variables. A smooth spline would imply values that cannot exist (like 10.3 words in a sentence, or a word ranked 890.6). The step shape is not a stylistic choice — it's what the data actually looks like.

Reading the charts for yourself

Next time you run the analyzer, hover over any point on either curve. The coordinates tell you a precise statistical fact about your writing. And when you compare two curves side by side, the differences between those coordinate pairs are the stylistic gap. That's the whole game.
